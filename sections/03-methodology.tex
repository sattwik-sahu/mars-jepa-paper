\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

In this work, we study the downstream performance on segementation tasks for two JEPAs trained with different tokenization masking strategies, on multispectral images.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:methodology_problem-formulation}

Consider a multispectral image \( I \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \), where \( C \) is the fixed number of bands, \( H, W \) are the height and width of the image respectively. We define a binary segmentation mask \( M \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W} \) over the pixels in the image, where a pixel \( M[i, j] \) at row $i$ and column $j$ in the segmentation mask \( M \) has a value of $1$ if it is predicted to be affected by a landslide, and $0$ otherwise.

The Martian landslide detection problem is to develop a segmentation model \( S_{\gamma}: \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W} \to \{0, 1\}^{H \times W} \), parameterized by \( \gamma \), to satisfy the objective function
\begin{equation}
	\underset{\gamma}{\max\ }{\mathbb{E}_{(I, M) \sim D}\left[
	\sigma_\mathrm{sim}(S_\gamma(I), M)
	\right]},
	\label{eq:ls-seg-obj}
\end{equation}
where the multispectral image and corresponding segmentation mask pairs \( (I, M) \) are sampled from the dataset \( D \), and \( \sigma_{\mathrm{sim}}(M_1, M_2) \in \mathbb{R} \) is a similarity metric between two binary segmentation masks \( M_1 \) and \( M_2 \). The aim is to maximize the expected similarity between the predicted segmentation mask and the actual segmentation mask, for all examples in the dataset, by performing an optimization over the model parameters \( \gamma \).


\subsection{Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA)}
\label{sec:methodology_jepa}

The Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) trains an encoder by applying inductive biases to maximize the information content about the input. In the context of multispectral image encoding, it does so by training an encoder to extract such features from a perturbed version of the image, that enable a lighter predictor module to predict the features of the unperturbed version of the image, conditioned on the perturbation applied to image to form the context. The perturbed image is called the context \( x \), the unperturbed image is called the target \( y \), and the respective features extracted by the encoder \( E_\theta \) are denoted by \( s_x \) and \( s_y \). The predictor module, denoted by \( D_\phi \), conditioned on the perturbation \( z \), outputs a prediction for the target features, denoted by \( \hat{s}_y \). Mathematically,
\begin{align}
	s_x                       & = E_\theta(x)           \label{eq:enc-x}                                                  \\
	s_y                       & = E_\theta(y)           \label{eq:enc-y}                                                  \\
	\hat{s}_y                 & = D_\phi(s_x, z)        \label{eq:pred-y}                                                 \\
	\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{pred} & = \mathcal{D}(\hat{s}_y, s_y) \label{eq:pred-loss}                                        \\
	\theta^\ast, \phi^\ast    & = \underset{\theta, \phi}{\arg\min\ }{\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{pred}}. \label{eq:train-search}
\end{align}
In this formulation, \cref{eq:enc-x,eq:enc-y} define the latent encoding of the context and target signals. \Cref{eq:pred-y} describes the predictive step in the latent space. The discrepancy between the predicted and actual target embeddings is quantified in \cref{eq:pred-loss} by a distance metric $\mathcal{D}(\cdot, \cdot)$. Finally, \cref{eq:train-search} defines the optimization objective, where the parameters $\theta$ and $\phi$ are learned by minimizing the prediction error in the latent space. Finally, to prevent latent space collapse in the encoder, we apply the SIGReg regularization~\cite{balestriero2025lejepaprovablescalableselfsupervised} loss to both, the context and target features, \( s_x \) and \( s_y \), calculating the total loss as
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{total} = (1 - \lambda) \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{pred} + \lambda (\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SIGReg}(s_x) + \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{SIGReg}(s_y)),
	\label{eq:combined-loss}
\end{equation}
where \( \lambda \in [0, 1] \) is a hyperparameter. In \cref{eq:pred-loss}, we use the $L_2$ divergence as the predictive loss divergence metric $\mathcal{D}$.

\subsection{I-JEPA Baseline for Multispectral Image Encoding}
\label{sec:methodology_ijepa}

We benchmark the I-JEPA~\cite{assran2023selfsupervisedlearningimagesjointembedding} framework for learning semantic representations for multispectral images, treating multispectral images like regular RGB images with extra channels. As shown in \cref{fig:ijepa}, the context \( x \) is constructed by applying the same patch-wise mask to all channels, while the target \( y \) is the unmasked image, containing information from all patches and channels. We implement the encoder \( E_\theta \) as a vision transformer (ViT)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021an} with learnable patch positional embeddings. The predictor is implemented as a transformer decoder with the positional embeddings of the masked patches as the query and the encodings for the visible patches as the key and value. The output is the prediction for encodings of the masked patches. The predictive loss is calculated by matching the predicted and actual encodings for the masked patches.

\subsection{Spatio-Spectral JEPA (SS-JEPA)}
\label{sec:methodology_ssjepa}

To learn stronger cross-modal dependencies in the semantic representations, we introduce Spatio-Spectral JEPA (SS-JEPA), which implements channel-wise masking and tokenization in contrast to the common mask and tokenization strategy across all channels, from \cref{sec:methodology_ijepa}. As shown in \cref{fig:ssjepa}, SS-JEPA uses distinct masks per channel, and the encoder outputs patch encodings for each visible patch in each channel. The encoder \( E_\theta \) is implemented as a modified ViT that tokenizes each patch in each channel, unlike the common tokenizer for all channels in I-JEPA. The predictor is a transformer decoder, with the spatial-spectral positional embeddings of the masked patches as queries and the encodings for the visible patches as input. The output is the prediction for the encodings of the masked patches. The predictive loss is calculated as the $L_2$ divergence between the predicted and actual encodings for the masked patches.

Note, here, that a ``patch'' above is associated with a particular channel, unlike I-JEPA. Additionally, the spatial-spectral positional embeddings for each patch is obtained by adding the patch positional embedding for that patch with the corresponding \textit{channel} embedding for that patch. Mathematically,
\begin{equation}
	p_{c,j} = \mathbf{P}_\mathrm{spatial}[j] + \mathbf{P}_\mathrm{spectral}[c],
	\label{eq:ssjepa-posemb}
\end{equation}
where \( p_{c,j} \) is the spatial-spectral positional embedding for channel \( c \), for the spatial location indexed by \( j \). Here, \( \mathbf{P}_\mathrm{spatial} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_p \times d} \) and \( \mathbf{P}_\mathrm{spectral} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_c \times d} \) are the spatial and spectral embedding matrices respectively, and \( n_p \) and \( n_c \) are the number of patches and channels in each image, respectively. In our dataset, there are \( n_c = 7 \) bands in the multispectral images.

\subsection{Segmentation Models with Self-Supervised Backbones}
\label{sec:methodology_segmentation}
Following the self-supervised pre-training phase, we integrate the JEPA encoders with a supervised segmentation decoder head to construct a segmentation model. As illustrated in \cref{fig:seg-integ}, the primary distinction between the two architectures lies in the nature of the latent features provided to the segmentation head  \( S_{\text{seg}}\). While I-JEPA generates spectrally aggregated encodings, SS-JEPA preserves the modality-specific information through a channel-wise feature representation. To facilitate this integration, we construct a multiscale feature pyramid with features
% \( f_0, f_1, \ldots, f_5 \), using the following operations: let $\mathcal{P}^{K \times K}_\mathrm{max}(\cdot)$ denotes the max pooling operation with kernel size \(k\) and stride \(k\). The feature pyramid levels are defined as:
\begin{align}
	f_0 & = I                                            \\
	f_1 & = \mathcal{P}^\mathrm{max}_{2 \times 2}(f_0)   \\
	f_2 & = \mathcal{P}^\mathrm{max}_{2 \times 2}(f_1)   \\
	f_3 & = \mathrm{E}_\theta(I)                         \\
	f_4 & = \mathcal{P}^\mathrm{max}_{2 \times 2}(f_3)   \\
	f_5 & = \mathcal{P}^\mathrm{max}_{2 \times 2}(f_4) ,
\end{align}
where \( \mathcal{P}^\mathrm{max}_{2 \times 2} \) denotes a max-pooling operation with a \( 2 \times 2 \) kernel, \( I \) is an image sampled from the dataset, and \( E_\theta \) is the encoder being integrated.
In this hierarchy, \(f_0\), \(f_1\), and \(f_2\) serve as high resolution skip connections that preserve sharp topographic edges. The bottleneck layer, \(f_3\) is the point of divergence between the two pre-training strategies. In the I-JEPA path, the encoder treats the multispectral input as a unified spatial cube, resulting in a single feature map where spectral information is collapsed into the channel dimension whereas the SS-JEPA path maintains the integrity of the individual modalities. It outputs a set of \(n_c\) independent feature maps of shape \(d \times h \times w\) representing the encodings for each modality as a feature map, where for an image \( I \) of resolution \( H \times W \) pixels tokenized into patches of size \( p \times p \), \( h = H / p \) and \( w = W / p \). These maps are subsequently processed by a spectral fusion module \(W_{fuse}\) which fuses the encoding for a particular patch across all channels, resulting in a \( d \)-dimensional feature map of shape \( h \times w \). This spatio-spectral input ensures that the segmentation head \(S_{seg}\) recieves context-rich features that account for the cross-modal relations learned during pre-training. Finally, \(f_4\) and \(f_5\) provides the global context required for consistent lanslide identification across diverse martian surface.

The segmentation model is trained using a hybrid objective function that combines region-level overlap consistency with pixel-wise classification accuracy. To maximize the similarity between the predicted segmentation mask $\hat{M}$ and the ground-truth mask $M$, the overall segmentation loss is defined as

\begin{align}
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{seg}}  & = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}} \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} & = 1 - \frac{
		2 \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat{M}_i M_i + \epsilon
	}{
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat{M}_i
		+
		\sum_{i=1}^{N} M_i
		+
		\epsilon
	}                                                                                        \\
	\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}
	                            & =
	-
	\frac{1}{N}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N}
	\left[
		M_i \log(\hat{M}_i)
		+
		(1-M_i)\log(1-\hat{M}_i)
		\right],
\end{align}
where $\hat{M}_i \in [0,1]$ denotes the predicted probability for the $i^{\mathrm{th}}$ pixel belonging to the foreground class, while $M_i \in \{0,1\}$ represents the corresponding ground-truth binary label. The variable $N = HW$ denotes the total number of pixels in the segmentation mask. The Dice loss, denoted by $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}$, measures the structural overlap between the predicted and ground-truth masks, encouraging global region consistency. The binary cross-entropy loss, denoted by $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}$ evaluates pixel-wise classification accuracy by penalizing incorrect foreground and background predictions. The smoothing constant $\epsilon$ is introduced to avoid numerical instability and division-by-zero during optimization.

During the downstream segmentation task, the parameters of the encoder $E_{\theta}$ are kept frozen in order to evaluate the quality and transferability of the learned self-supervised representations. Only the parameters of the segmentation decoder are optimized using the supervised segmentation objective defined above. The supervised fine-tuning process is performed on the training subset of the MMLSv2 dataset~\cite{mmlsv2_2026}.

This two-stage training strategy, consisting of self-supervised pre-training followed by supervised segmentation, enables the model to effectively exploit the large volume of unlabeled Martian surface imagery. As a result, the learned representations improve the robustness and generalization capability of the final landslide detection framework, particularly in scenarios with limited annotated data.
